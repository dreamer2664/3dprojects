You are not being asked to make a clock-shaped prop. ## 1. THE EXACT DESIGN DIRECTION — DO NOT MAKE A GENERIC GRANDFATHER CLOCK

Create a **late-Victorian / turn-of-the-century English exhibition-quality mahogany longcase clock**, visually rich, stately and architecturally composed. The primary historical anchor is the **J. Smith & Sons longcase regulator clock exhibited at Paris in 1900**, catalogued by the National Trust at Castle Drogo (object NT 902584).

This is the essential design target. The National Trust record identifies a very large mahogany clock, 113 × 27 × 18 inches (approximately 287 × 69 × 46 cm), with gilt-brass mounts, a Chippendale-style case, break-arch brass dial, silvered chapter ring, moon-phase and lunar-calendar indication, glazed hood and sides, three brass-cased weights, and a multi-train chiming movement. Its hood is flanked by four caryatids and surmounted by four brass dragons and a female bust. The described movement has three trains, four pillars, a dead-beat escapement, mercury-compensated pendulum and quarter chimes.

**Make this object the visual and engineering north star.** Do not simplify it into a plain narrow case with a round clock face. Equally, do not randomly combine every ornamental feature from every other clock you find. The case, dial, carving, mounts and movement must read as one coherent commission made by one high-end London clockmaker around 1900.

Use the specialist photographed J. Smith & Sons circa-1900 clock as the secondary visual / mechanical comparison: its mahogany case has a swan-neck pediment, carved lion details, quarter pillars, Corinthian capitals, a glazed trunk door, elaborate applied brass fretwork, silvered chapter ring, subsidiary dials, large pendulum and a quarter-chiming tube arrangement. This source is valuable because it provides a multi-image gallery of a real clock from the same maker and period. It is a comparative object, not an instruction to paste its lion carving onto the National Trust design.

### Visual character

- Overall impression: substantial, prestigious, finely made, slightly theatrical, but still credible as a real English clock made for an important interior.
- Main timber: richly figured mahogany, with carefully selected veneer panels and solid timber structure.
- Metalwork: aged and selectively burnished gilt brass, not uniform yellow plastic or chrome.
- Glazing: real clear glass with thickness, edge reflections, subtle imperfections and correct reflections.
- Dial: complex and legible; layered metalwork, engraved detail, raised numerals, hands and subsidiary indications.
- Ornament: sculpted architectural and figurative carving, not flat decorative masks.
- Internal view: a genuinely convincing horological mechanism visible through the glazed trunk and side panels.

### Scale and proportions

Use the National Trust object dimensions as the primary scale anchor:

- Height: approximately **2.87 m**.
- Width: approximately **0.69 m**.
- Depth: approximately **0.46 m**.

Confirm proportions from all available images and refine them. Do not stretch a generic clock mesh to meet those numbers. Ensure the base, trunk, hood, glass openings, movement clearance and pendulum travel all work together physically.

## 3. MODEL THE CLOCK AS A REAL CONSTRUCTED OBJECT

### 3.1 Case architecture and joinery

Construct the case from meaningful separate boards, rails, stiles, panels, veneers, applied mouldings and structural supports. The case is not one monolithic mesh with a wood shader.

Model, at minimum:

- The architectural hood, pediment, cornice, entablature, friezes, moulded edges and stepped profiles.
- The four caryatid figures as proper three-dimensional sculptural forms with readable faces, hair, drapery, anatomy, undercuts and contact shadows.
- The four brass dragons and the central female bust as independent, high-resolution sculptural metal ornaments. They must be recognisable and carefully finished, not vaguely dragon-shaped blobs.
- The hood's dial surround, columns, capitals, bases, side sound frets / openings, glazing beads, rebates and door frame.
- A fully constructed trunk with vertical stiles, horizontal rails, panelled sections, glazed front door and glazed sides.
- The lower door, figured panel, mouldings, hinges, escutcheon, lock, keyhole, plinth, bracket feet and base mouldings.
- Every brass mount as separate geometry with realistic thickness and fixing points.
- Real panel joints, mitres, tenons, rabbets, rebates, pins, screws and concealed supports where they would plausibly exist.

Do not use generic bevel modifiers as a substitute for designing proper mouldings. Use authentic stepped profiles, cove, ogee, astragal, bead, reeded and gadrooned forms where reference-supported. Ease sharp edges subtly. Do not make the clock pristine and sterile: this is a preserved antique with a long life, not a new plastic replica.

### 3.2 The dial must be a miniature masterpiece

Treat the dial as its own major modelling project. It must reward a close-up camera crop.

- Build the break-arch dial plate with accurate thickness, perimeter profile and mounting structure.
- Create the silvered chapter ring, engraved minute divisions, Roman hour numerals and Arabic five-minute markers with clean, consistent typography and spacing.
- Model the lion-mask spandrels and the engraved / chased brass ornament in layered relief.
- Build the moon-phase arch, moon disc/globe and lunar calendar indication with coherent movement and period-appropriate markings.
- Model the subsidiary seconds, calendar, chime selector and strike/silent indications described by the selected reference. Do not invent a random assortment of tiny circles.
- Create the hands with plausible sheet-metal construction, blued steel colour, tapered profiles and accurate central collets.
- Model the chapter ring, dial boss, winding arbors, screws, bezel, glass and locking access as separate components.
- Use crisp, readable Roman numerals and lettering. If exact original wording is unknown, use restrained period typography and a plausible maker's cartouche, not gibberish pseudo-text.

At macro level, the dial should show engraved lines, tiny screw slots, subtle tarnish in recesses, polished touch points, slight surface variation, clean glass reflections and sharply resolved numerals.

### 3.3 Genuine clock mechanism — no decorative gear theatre

Build a mechanically intelligible **three-train, four-pillar, weight-driven clock movement** consistent with the National Trust 1900 reference. Research the exact arrangement before modelling it. The movement must be a visible, inspectable assembly, not a block hidden behind the dial.

Model the parts needed for the documented arrangement, including:

- Main movement plates with realistic thickness, chamfered edges, pillar spacing, nuts, screws, standoffs and mounting points.
- The going train: great wheel, intermediate wheels, pinions, arbors, escape wheel, dead-beat pallets, crutch and suspension connection.
- The strike/chime train(s), racks, snails, levers, gathering/pawl components, lifting pieces and control parts as appropriate to the chosen chiming system.
- The four pillars / movement spacers, bearing holes, pivots and end-shake details.
- Three independent brass-cased weights with suspension eyes and believable masses / proportions.
- Cables or chains following the actual route over correctly grooved pulleys. Model individual chain links if the chosen drive uses chain; do not paint a stripe onto the glass.
- A long pendulum with rod, suspension spring, crutch interface, compensation assembly, mercury jar / bob, rating adjustment and fixing details. If exact mercury-container details are uncertain, use a historically credible sealed compensation vessel and document the assumption.
- The described quarter-chime and hour-strike sounders: bells, gongs or tubes, supports, brackets and hammers. Make their location and strike linkage coherent.

The pendulum must swing freely without intersecting the case, door or weights. The chains must not clip through pulleys. The hands must align with the movement's hand arbor. Every gear must mesh or be explicitly a non-functional display element. Do not model gear teeth that overlap or fail to engage.

Provide an inspection state with the trunk door open and the mechanism visible. If feasible, animate the pendulum, hands, strike train and chime hammers. The animation must be mechanically plausible at the visual level; it does not need to simulate the actual clock rate, but it must not contradict the geometry.

## 4. THE MAHOGANY MUST LOOK LIKE REAL MAHOGANY

This is one of the two most important acceptance criteria. A brown glossy material is not wood realism.

- Research the particular mahogany species and period finishing practice. Do not confidently claim species-level identification if the reference only says “mahogany”.
- Use coherent grain direction for every board. Vertical case stiles, horizontal rails, door panels, mouldings, veneers, feet and carved pieces must each have appropriate grain orientation.
- Model solid timber and veneered surfaces differently. Veneer must have a plausible thickness and substrate; bookmatching or figured veneer must be deliberate, not a random swirl.
- Create separate board components with visible thickness, end grain, joints, panel gaps, slight board-to-board variation and subtle hand-worked irregularity.
- Use real photographed wood references / scans or carefully authored high-resolution wood maps. Do not rely exclusively on Blender Noise Texture, Voronoi or generic procedural brown streaks.
- Provide separate colour, roughness, normal, height/displacement and, where useful, cavity / bump maps. Use 8K maps for main visible case surfaces; 4K is the absolute floor for secondary wood areas. Use higher resolution where a close-up demonstrably needs it.
- Avoid obvious texture repetition, stretched grain and the same texture copied identically across every panel.
- Reproduce natural figure, pores, rays where appropriate, grain contrast, tiny surface depressions, polishing marks and the way finish gathers around profiles and carved recesses.
- Build a plausible antique finish stack: timber, stain, sealer / shellac or period-compatible polish, wax or later maintenance polish, accumulated handling polish, and age-related variation. Research it; do not apply an implausible modern plastic clearcoat.
- Add restrained age evidence: faint hairline scratches, softened corners, minute dents, slightly darker grime in protected recesses, handling polish around doors and brass, and a few subtle variations in patina. No exaggerated grunge, rot, missing chunks or fantasy damage.

**Close-up test:** render the front trunk panel, a carved mahogany moulding, and a figured veneer area at 1:1 or near 1:1 output scale. They must read as separate pieces of finished timber, not a flat image wrapped over a smooth box.

## 5. METAL, GLASS, PAINT AND MICRODETAIL

- Gilt brass mounts need actual thickness, folded / cast profiles, backs, pins, screws and subtle tool marks. Use variation between burnished high points and oxidised recesses.
- Brass dial plates need engraved or chased relief, edge wear, fine scratches and realistic metallic response. Avoid uniform yellow.
- Steel parts should be dark, blued or polished according to function. Pivots and wear surfaces may show brighter burnished contact points.
- Glass panels need real thickness, believable edge treatment, subtle waviness and reflections. Keep the movement visible; do not turn the glass into a mirror.
- Dial glass and bezel must fit their openings properly and have plausible retaining clips / beads.
- Felt pads, leather interfaces, paper labels, wood shims and gaskets should appear only where mechanically and historically plausible.
- Screws, nuts, washers, rivets, hinge pins and keyholes must be correctly scaled and oriented. No floating hardware.

## 6. GEOMETRY AND TEXTURE BUDGET — DO NOT SELF-CENSOR

This is a flagship high-detail master, not a real-time game asset. There is **no arbitrary maximum polygon count** and no need to target mobile performance. Do not decimate, flatten, bake away or omit visible detail merely to make the object lighter.

- **Minimum geometry target: 30 million evaluated triangles** across the complete detailed master scene, excluding cameras, lights and any non-rendering helper curves. Aim for **50–70 million or more** if justified by the carving, dial, movement and hardware. There is no upper cap.
- The triangle count must represent actual rendered geometry, not a claimed procedural estimate or hidden instancing count that is absent from the exported asset.
- Fine carving, figurative sculptures, fretwork, gear teeth, chain links, screw threads, engraved relief and ornamental castings deserve genuine geometric resolution.
- Use displacement / subdivision / sculpting for appropriate surfaces. Preserve all high-resolution source meshes in the `.blend`.
- Major wood surfaces: 8K textures minimum where feasible. Dial, carved ornament and hero metalwork: 4K–8K or higher as needed. Do not force every component into one texture atlas if that destroys texel density.
- Create distinct texture sets for wood, brass/gilt metal, steel, dial, glass / transparent elements and soft materials.
- No fake detail created by multiplying noise or adding random scratches. All wear must be scale-aware and placed according to use, contact and age.

If available memory becomes a problem, split high-detail components into separately linked collections / scene layers, render in sections, use proxy geometry only for preview and keep the full-resolution final source. Do not silently reduce the required detail. Record any true environment constraint and the workaround.
